\section{What was built}

A single pipeline serving both datasets through one internal schema:
\texttt{build\_pipeline.py} runs download $\rightarrow$ parse $\rightarrow$ temporal split
$\rightarrow$ feature store in one command. MIND (English TSV, 130{,}379 articles) and
EB-NeRD (Danish parquet, 125{,}541 articles) normalise to identical \texttt{articles} and
\texttt{impressions} frames, so every downstream component is written once. Two retrievers
sit on top: \textbf{BM25} (own inverted index, $k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$, Snowball stemming
per language) and \textbf{semantic} (MiniLM sentence embeddings, 384-d, FAISS
inner-product over $L_2$-normalised vectors). A shared harness reports AUC/MRR/nDCG with
bootstrap CIs, plus diversity/novelty/coverage and slicing.

The task has \emph{two} shapes and they reward opposite things --- a distinction that drove
most of the analysis below. \textbf{Retrieval} pulls top-$K$ from the whole catalogue
(recall@$K$). \textbf{Re-ranking} orders the ${\sim}12$--$40$ candidates an impression
already contains (AUC/MRR/nDCG); this is what Codabench scores.

\keybox{\textbf{Semantic wins re-ranking on both datasets; BM25 wins retrieval on
EB-NeRD.} Taking either dataset alone yields a confident, wrong general conclusion about
which family is better.}

\subsection{(a) EB-NeRD BM25 is reliably worse than random.}
AUC 0.4768, CI $[0.4755, 0.4781]$ --- the interval excludes 0.5, so this is systematic
anti-correlation, not noise. Within a ${\sim}12$-candidate list from one publisher on one
day, the articles sharing most vocabulary with a 267-article history are the long, generic
ones, which are not what gets clicked. BM25's length normalisation corrects for document
length but not for \emph{topical genericness}.

\subsection{(b) Longer history helps ranking and hurts retrieval --- every time.}
Moving from last-10 clicks to full history: MIND BM25 AUC $0.5544\!\rightarrow\!0.5624$,
MIND semantic $0.6194\!\rightarrow\!0.6324$, EB-NeRD semantic
$0.5469\!\rightarrow\!0.5574$; but MIND BM25 recall@50 fell $0.0118\!\rightarrow\!0.0089$
and EB-NeRD semantic recall@50 fell $0.0008\!\rightarrow\!0.0004$. A longer query matches
more documents at least weakly, diluting a whole-catalogue ranking, while adding usable
signal when ordering an already-curated list. There is no single correct history length for
a system doing both.

\subsection{(c) The head/tail effect is not universal.}
Tail AUC exceeds head in three of four dataset$\times$retriever cells (MIND semantic 0.6566
vs 0.6300; MIND BM25 0.6034 vs 0.5584; EB-NeRD BM25 0.5218 vs 0.4718) and \emph{reverses}
in the fourth (EB-NeRD semantic 0.5176 vs 0.5535). Three of four was enough to write it up
as a general property; the fourth cell refuted it. Note too that MIND tail AUC rises while
tail MRR \emph{falls} (0.2677 vs 0.3451) --- AUC asks ``can you separate?'', MRR asks ``is
it at rank 1?''. Tuning on AUC alone would make a system look good on the long tail while
serving it badly.

\section{Index and tooling choices, measured against alternatives}

MIND corpus $130{,}379\times384$-d. Latency is single-query (serving shape); recall is
measured \emph{against the exact result} --- i.e.\ does this index return what exhaustive
search would? \texttt{python -m src.evaluation.benchmark\_index}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{l r r r r r r}
\toprule
\textbf{FAISS index} & \textbf{build s} & \textbf{size MB} & \textbf{lat.\ ms} & \textbf{p95} & \textbf{batch q/s} & \textbf{recall@100}\\
\midrule
\textbf{IndexFlatIP (shipped)} & 0.07 & 200.3 & 2.704 & 3.67 & 5{,}151 & 1.0000\\
IVFFlat nprobe=1   & 2.66 & 202.9 & 0.055 & 0.079 & 102{,}699 & \textcolor{bad}{0.3728}\\
IVFFlat nprobe=8   & 2.24 & 202.9 & 0.094 & 0.120 & 38{,}834  & 0.7550\\
IVFFlat nprobe=32  & 2.32 & 202.9 & 0.229 & 0.286 & 13{,}653  & 0.9060\\
HNSW efSearch=32   & 9.31 & 235.8 & 0.290 & 0.455 & 22{,}666  & 0.8312\\
HNSW efSearch=128  & 8.96 & 235.8 & 0.617 & 0.881 & 11{,}318  & 0.9644\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

IVF at \texttt{nprobe=1} is \textbf{49$\times$ faster} than exact search and discards
\textbf{63\% of true neighbours} --- the trap in quoting a speedup without its recall.
Raising \texttt{nprobe} walks that back; HNSW dominates IVF on accuracy at comparable
latency (96.4\% agreement, 4.4$\times$ faster) for $+35$\,MB and ${\sim}130\times$ the build
time.

\keybox{\textbf{Why we still ship exact search:} the serving path never queries the index.
Codabench supplies the candidate list, so submissions go through
\texttt{score\_candidates()}, which scores the named ${\sim}12$--$40$ articles directly.
FAISS is exercised only by offline recall@$K$, where 5{,}151 q/s is ample. An approximate
index would trade 4--63\% of true neighbours for latency we have no use for, and would make
recall@$K$ a measurement of \emph{approximation error} rather than of retrieval quality. On
a serving path that did hit the index per request, HNSW \texttt{efSearch=128} is where we
would go.}

\subsection{Storage.}
FAISS index 200.3\,MB $\cdot$ embeddings 200.3\,MB $\cdot$ \textbf{BM25 index 32.2\,MB}
$\cdot$ article catalogue 29.1\,MB. BM25's index is \textbf{6$\times$ smaller}, builds in
seconds from raw text, and needs no model download or GPU --- the honest argument in its
favour even though it loses on accuracy here.

\section{Optimising the scoring path}

1{,}000 MIND impressions; mean history 33.8, mean candidates 40.6.
\texttt{python -m src.evaluation.benchmark\_retrieval}

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{l r r l}
\toprule
\textbf{Scoring path} & \textbf{ms/impr.} & \textbf{per second} & \textbf{Outcome}\\
\midrule
BM25 \texttt{score\_candidates}      & 0.528  & 1{,}894  & shipped\\
Semantic \texttt{score\_candidates}  & 0.051  & 19{,}608 & shipped\\
BM25 scoring full corpus             & 14.111 & 71       & rejected --- 27$\times$\\
Semantic scoring full corpus         & 3.875  & 258      & rejected --- 76$\times$\\
BM25 without token memoisation       & 2.681  & 373      & rejected --- 5.1$\times$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Targeted vs full-corpus scoring} is the single most expensive available mistake:
scoring the whole catalogue and reading off the candidates gives identical rankings at
27$\times$ (BM25) and 76$\times$ (semantic) the cost --- roughly 53 hours versus 2 on
EB-NeRD's 13.5\,M-impression test set. \textbf{Token memoisation} gives 5.1$\times$ on BM25:
article tokens never change, but the original code re-tokenised and re-\emph{stemmed} each
user's entire history on every impression, which measured at 77\% of all work the method did.

\keybox{\textbf{Two optimisations were pessimisations until measured.} Reusing a
vocabulary-width IDF buffer (to avoid an $O(V)$ allocation per call) made BM25
\emph{slower}: 0.938\,ms vs 0.717\,ms for plain \texttt{np.zeros}. Vectorising the semantic
candidate gather likewise went $0.062\rightarrow0.077$\,ms. Both arguments were asymptotic
and both were wrong: \texttt{np.zeros} is a C-level calloc with lazily-zeroed pages, so the
memset was nearly free, while the Python loop added to \emph{clear} the buffer ran over
every unique query term. Rewriting both with NumPy fancy-indexing instead of Python loops
gave the real win --- \textbf{0.147\,ms} and \textbf{0.034\,ms}, i.e.\ 4.9$\times$ and
1.8$\times$ over the originals. The gain came from vectorising index construction, not from
avoiding allocation. Outputs verified identical (BM25 max abs diff 0.0; semantic
$8.9\mathrm{e}{-}08$ = float32 epsilon).}

\subsection{Throughput at submission scale.}
MIND 2{,}370{,}727 impressions: semantic 2\,m\,13\,s (17{,}790/s), BM25 8\,m\,38\,s
(4{,}574/s). EB-NeRD 13{,}536{,}710: semantic ${\approx}18$\,min (${\approx}12{,}400$/s),
peak RSS flat at 3.8\,GB.

\section{Correctness work that mattered more than tuning}

\begin{tightitem}
\item \textbf{Wrong dataset bundle.} \texttt{preprocess\_mind()} checked
\texttt{MINDsmall\_} before \texttt{MINDlarge\_}; both were on disk, so the whole pipeline
silently ran on MIND-small while the test set comes from MIND-large.
\item \textbf{Stale embeddings.} The on-disk index still held 125{,}589 MIND-small vectors
and was being reused against the 130{,}379-article catalogue, so many users' history
articles were simply absent and their profiles degraded toward random.
\item \textbf{Unloadable index cache.} Pickling the live \texttt{FastBM25} object recorded
its class as \texttt{\_\_main\_\_.FastBM25} when built via \texttt{python -m}, so
\texttt{generate\_preds.py} and \texttt{metrics.py} could not unpickle it at all --- a
submission run would have crashed. Now persisted as plain arrays.
\item \textbf{Caches blind to configuration.} Fingerprints covered the article-ID set but
not \emph{how} data was processed; added \texttt{TOKENIZER\_VERSION} and
\texttt{EMBEDDING\_MODEL\_VERSION}.
\end{tightitem}

These, not parameter tuning, produced the leaderboard movement (\S7). \textbf{An unverified
model swap was rejected:} \texttt{multilingual-e5-base} was implemented with correct
\texttt{query:}/\texttt{passage:} prefixing, but its download stalled and the comparison
never completed, so the measured MiniLM configuration stayed rather than shipping an
unmeasured change.

\subsection{Evaluation methodology.}
EB-NeRD's val split is ${\sim}2$\,M impressions and a full Q4 pass costs ${\approx}1$\,h per
retriever, so Q4 there uses a seeded 250{,}000 sample. \textbf{The sampling was validated,
not assumed:} the identical configuration run both ways gives AUC 0.5499 $[0.5486, 0.5511]$
at $N{=}250{,}000$ versus 0.5496 $[0.5493, 0.5501]$ at $N{=}1{,}997{,}301$ --- a difference
of 0.0003, inside both intervals.

\subsection{EB-NeRD has no cold-start slice.}
Its history file guarantees $\geq5$ clicks per user (\texttt{min\_history\_len = 5}, per the
provided notebook), so a $<5$-click threshold selects nobody and ``warm'' is identical to
``all''. Cold-start behaviour is therefore only studiable on MIND, where semantic degrades
more gracefully than BM25 ($0.6414\!\rightarrow\!0.5730$ vs $0.5673\!\rightarrow\!0.5303$):
one clicked article still yields a usable 384-d direction, but very few query terms.

\section{Anti-gaming}

\textbf{No feature unavailable at serving time is used.} Inputs are article text and the
user's prior clicks only. We deliberately exclude popularity,
\texttt{total\_inviews}/\texttt{pageviews}, \texttt{read\_time}, \texttt{scroll\_percentage}
and \texttt{next\_*} --- the last of which EB-NeRD itself withholds from its test set
because they are post-hoc. The ``with versus without'' comparison therefore degenerates:
there is no ``with'' configuration to report.

\textbf{The behaviour-window test previously did not exist.} The test module's docstring
claimed to assert that \texttt{click\_history} contains only pre-impression clicks, but no
test touched \texttt{click\_history}. Two real ones replaced the claim: EB-NeRD is checked
directly against article publication timestamps (5{,}437{,}152 history entries, 0
violations); MIND has no publication timestamps at all, so it is checked structurally --- a
user's history may only grow over time (6{,}288 multi-impression users, 0 violations).
Suite: 9 passed, 1 correctly skipped.

\section{Where it breaks at 10$\times$}

\begin{tightitem}
\item \textbf{Output materialisation (fixed).} Prediction lines were accumulated in memory
before writing --- 2.8\,GB for MIND's 2.37\,M lines, ${\sim}15$\,GB projected for EB-NeRD.
Now streamed straight into the zip; peak RSS is flat in row count.
\item \textbf{Exact search is the next wall.} Flat search is $O(N)$ per query. At
10$\times$ the catalogue (1.3\,M articles) recall@$K$ goes from ${\sim}31$\,min to
${\sim}5$\,h, and the index from 200\,MB to 2\,GB --- past comfortable RAM. This is exactly
when the \S3 table stops being academic: HNSW at 96.4\% agreement becomes the right trade.
\item \textbf{BM25 memory is superlinear in practice.} The sparse matrix is built from
Python lists of \texttt{(row, col, val)} triples before conversion to CSR --- fine at
130\,K docs, but at 10$\times$ the transient list overhead dominates. Building CSR
incrementally per row-group would fix it.
\item \textbf{Mean-pooling degrades as histories lengthen.} Already visible: EB-NeRD's
267-article histories give recall@50 of 0.0004 versus MIND's 0.0125 at 34. A
max-similarity variant scored better in ablation (0.6402 vs 0.6325 AUC) but has no single
query vector and so cannot be an ANN query --- it would force different scoring for
retrieval and re-ranking, which is why it was not shipped.
\item \textbf{Single-machine assumption.} Everything is one process; nothing shards. The
natural split is by impression (embarrassingly parallel) with a shared read-only index.
\end{tightitem}
